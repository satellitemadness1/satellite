# M19.5 — a run of bits, and the width that makes it a type

**Landed in two halves: binary 2026-09-08** (`09ac09c`)**, hex 2026-09-09**
(`cf04994`)**.**

**THE SPLIT WAS THE AUTHOR'S AND IT PAID.** PLAN §8's M19.5 is "binary and
hexadecimal" and claims two numbered paths. The author called the split
mid-milestone — *"Let's just do b1100 for right now, THEN we will do hex after
this"* — and the reason it was the right call is §2 below: the shape of the type
was decided four times over the first sitting, and every one of those decisions
would have had to be taken twice if both radices had been in flight.

**What the second half then cost is the measure of it.** §5 is the whole of it:
three questions that were genuinely new to hex, one of which turned an
unbuildable row into a buildable one, and nothing re-argued. Everything §2
settled was inherited without a word.

**AND THE OPERATORS ARE NOT IN THIS MILESTONE**, though every one of them was
decided on 2026-09-09 — see §6. That was the author's call once the size of the
operator set became visible, and the sentence that made it visible is worth
keeping: *"we forgot about order of operations like completely"*.

## 1. What is built

**Two numbered paths and six children each**, and the two tables line up row for
row — `1 6 5 n` and `1 6 11 n` ask the same question for every n:

| | `satellite.variable.binary` `1 6 5 (0)` | `satellite.variable.hex` `1 6 11 (0)` |
|---|---|---|
| | *2026-09-08* | *2026-09-09; also spelled `hexadecimal`* |
| `.to_number()` `n=1` | what the bits are WORTH — `b1010` → 10 | `x00FF` → 255 |
| `.width()` `n=2` | how many BITS — `b00001010` → 8 | `x00FF` → **16**, bits and not digits |
| `.to_string()` `n=3` | the characters `display` prints, `b` and all | `"x00FF"`, `x` and all |
| `.as_number()` | `1 6 5 4` — the digits read as decimal, `b1010` → 1010 | **no such row** — §5.2 |
| `.digits()` | `1 6 5 5` — 4, always `width()` here | `1 6 11 4` — 4 |
| the other radix | `1 6 5 6` `.to_hex()` → `xA`; refuses a ragged width | `1 6 11 5` `.to_binary()`; never refuses |

The `(0)` on each is new with its children. **The two tables line up for the
first three and then deliberately do not**, because hex has no `as_number()`:
§5.2 is why, and it is the one thing in this milestone that was built and then
removed.

**And the seventh and eighth arms of `Value`.** `Bin` is a `shared_ptr<const
bits::BitRun>` and `Hex` a `shared_ptr<const bits::HexRun>`, sixteen bytes each
against the 32-byte widest arm, appended at the end per value.hpp's append-only
rule. The 40-byte `static_assert` did not move for either.

***PLAN §8's entry calls these "the sixth and seventh appends" and that is off
by one.*** It was written on 2026-09-08 while M19 was in flight, and M19's `Fil`
took sixth. Binary is the seventh and hex the eighth. The half of the sentence
that mattered — *"the assert does not move"* — was right both times.

**`Hex` is a separate arm and not a radix flag on `Bin`**, which is what makes
`b1111 == xF` false without any code asking: different arms never compare equal,
and §8's model had already promised that answer. A shared arm would have made
the pair compare EQUAL unless every reader remembered to check a field.

**`write(x)` `1 6 2 11` gained its second and third arms and no new spelling**,
which is the whole reason PLAN put this milestone directly after M19. Until it
did, `write(x)` accepted only a string and read as `write_line` with the newline
left off — the entry predicted exactly that and it was true for one day.
`write(x41425243)` puts four bytes, `ABRC`, and `write(x415)` is refused at
twelve bits.

## 2. What was decided, and how many times

**This was a design milestone and PLAN said so**: DESIGN §8.5 specified the
literals and the width rule *and nothing else*, and listed five questions
"somebody has to write". Four of the five were settled by argument; the fifth
turned out to be already answered by code.

**The width is part of the value, and that is the only clause a representation
can silently break.** `b0010` and `b10` are two values, `b0010 == b10` is false,
and display prints what was written. *Every other fixture in the section would
still pass if a bit run were an integer with a length beside it* — so the three
that pin the width apart are the section, and the note in `tests/eval_test/bits.cpp`
says so where somebody deleting one would read it.

**The bits are `satellite.variable.bool`, one per bit, and the author asked for
a measurement before agreeing to it** — *"you may have to run a test and see if
you can store the binary numbers as bool's, maybe you can if it's not too much
memory"*. Taken, one million bits:

| | bytes |
|---|---|
| `std::vector<bool>` — the bit-packed specialisation | **125,000** |
| one byte per bit | 1,000,000 |
| one `Value` per bit | 40,000,000 |

**The worry was real and pointed at the wrong row.** A `satellite.variable.bool`
*is* the C++ `bool` in this tree — value.hpp's variant names it as the second
arm and the language has no other — so "store them as bools" and
`std::vector<bool>` are the same instruction, and the packing makes it the
cheapest option rather than the dearest. The 320× row is what "a vector of
`satellite.variable.bool`" costs if each one keeps its own type tag, and that is
the shape the question was really about.

**Hex will be stored as bits too and converted at display**, the author's, for
the reason that makes it exact: a hex digit is four bits and the round trip
loses nothing.

### 2.1 `to_number` and `as_number` were reversed, twice, and the second reversal is the one with an argument under it

The two conversions went in as `to_number()` → 10 and `as_number()` → 1010; the
author swapped them — *"b1010 'as a number' is 10, and b1010 'to a number' is
1010"* — then asked which way it should be, and swapped them back.

**What settled it was not English.** Both readings of "as a number" are natural,
which is why leaning on them produced two answers. The two arguments that do not
depend on taste:

- **`to_` is already this language's conversion verb.**
  `satellite.variable.string.to_number` `1 6 1 13` turns `"42"` into 42 and
  `satellite.variable.number.to_string` `1 6 4 6` goes back, so a `to_` row
  answers the same value wearing the other type. Every other language answers 10
  when asked for an integer out of `1010` base two.
- **The surprising answer must not sit behind the expected name.** A reader of
  `b1010.to_number()` expects 10; a row that quietly answered 1010 would be a
  wrong answer that looks right, which is DESIGN §1.1 in the one place it is
  hardest to notice.

**What the flip cost, and what stopped it costing more.** The C++ functions were
first named `to_number` and `as_number` to mirror the two rows, so the first swap
was a rewiring rather than an edit. They are now `value_of()` and
`digits_as_number()` — named for what they compute — and `bits_methods.cpp` is
the only place the two vocabularies meet. bits.hpp carries the reason where the
next person would otherwise re-introduce the mirror.

### 2.2 A method needs a number; an operator does not

PLAN asked for **"two numbered paths and no third"**, and the four rows above
sit *under* `1 6 5` rather than beside it. That line is what made it possible to
answer the plan's questions without minting anything: `+`, `[` and the shifts
are operators and cost no path number at all.

**So M19.5 built none of them, deliberately**, and DESIGN §8.5 now records each
as open with the argument that will decide it. The author's sketch for `+` — one
operator for concatenation and a second, `!!`, for arithmetic addition — is
written down there rather than built, because arithmetic on a width-carrying
value needs a carry rule and an answer-width, which is precisely the sentence
this milestone exists to not write in a margin.

**Asked and answered by argument:** `b1010 == b00001010` is false (the width);
`b1010 == xF` will be false when hex lands (different arms never compare equal,
which §8's no-conversion model already decided).

**Asked and answered by the code that was already there:** *which of the two the
`.satc` writes*. SATC §3's "literals stay literal" governs it and
`write_expressions.cpp` already printed the token verbatim — `b1010` — with the
`b` doing the work of saying which type. Nothing had to change, and the question
was only open because nobody had looked.

### 2.3 `write(x)` refuses a width that is not a multiple of eight

A file is made of bytes and half a byte cannot be written. Padding to the next
byte and left-aligning in the last one both write bits the program never wrote,
so the refusal is the only answer that invents nothing — and it is one a program
can act on, `width()` being the question it would ask. Writing the *text* is
spelled `write(x.to_string())`, out loud.

**And the bytes are the bytes.** `write(b0100000101000010)` puts `41 42` and not
the seventeen characters of the literal, which is the difference that would be
invisible until something read the file back.

## 3. What building it found

**A method on a literal does not compile, and the entry predicted it.** PLAN
§8's M19.5 entry carries two working notes "to know before writing a line of
it"; the first is that a selector folds only through a declared name
(WORD_NUMBERS §1.5's one hop), and that *"this milestone will feel it more than
most, because a conversion verb is exactly the shape people chain"*.

**It was right, and it was found the way the note says it would be** — not by
reading the note, but by eight test fixtures failing at once, every one of them
a method called on `b1010` directly. `example/binary.satl` hit it twice more,
including on `run.width().to_string()`, which is the chain the note names.

The fixtures now name the receiver, **and one of them pins the refusal** so the
rule is proved rather than worked around. No milestone owns loosening it and
this one did not try.

**A `satellite.variable.bool` has no `to_string()`.** Found writing the example,
which wanted to join `b1010 == b00001010`'s answer onto a sentence and could
not. It is a real gap and it is one type over; the example displays the bool
instead and says why on the line above it.

**WORD_NUMBERS §2.2 used this very node as the example for a rule, and the
example was wrong about the reason.** The paragraph said `1 6 5` has no `(0)`
"because nothing calls it with no arguments — it is a type name, and the number
is the whole of it". But `string` `1 6 1 0`, `file` `1 6 2 0`, `time` `1 6 3 0`,
`number` `1 6 4 0`, `thread` `1 6 13 0` and `variant` `1 6 14 0` are all type
names and all carry one. **A type name's `(0)` is the BARE SHAPE** — a declared
name holding nothing, `satellite.variable.binary bits` with no `=` after it —
and binary had none because it had no children to hang them under. The rule did
not change; §2.2 now states it in the form that covers both kinds of word.

## 4. What proves it

**`tests/eval_test/bits.cpp` at nineteen checks and `tests/eval_test/hex.cpp`
at twenty-one**, and the ones that matter most are those an integer
representation would fail. The mutations:

- **Trim a leading zero anywhere** in `satellite_bits/` — `b00001010` displays as
  `b1010`, `width()` answers 4, and `b00001010 == b1010` becomes true. Three
  checks, and DESIGN §8.5's whole claim is gone.
- **Swap `value_of` and `digits_as_number`** in `bits_methods.cpp` — the two
  conversion checks fail, which is the fixture pair standing in for §2.1's
  decision.
- **Drop a row from the install loop** — the count check fails: 48 handlers, up
  from 37 before this milestone and from 41 after its first half. *That check
  is not in `bits.cpp`* — it is the one `section_scalars`, `section_variant` and
  `section_containers` already each carry over the summed install, and the
  eleven new rows moved all three, three times: 41, then 49, then 48 when
  hex's `as_number` came out. A count nobody can add to without touching three
  files is the point of writing it three times.
- **Accept a ragged width in `to_bytes`** — `file_test` still passes, because it
  does not know about bits; the check that fails is the refusal fixture here.

**`help_lines/verify.py` runs 182 worked examples against `./satl` and all 182
pass**, fifteen of them this milestone's. The help entries are not prose about
the type — they are programs that were run.

**`nodes.tsv` was re-measured rather than typed, for the first half.** The five
new rows were written by hand to get the tree building, then the enumerator was
rebuilt against the new objects and its output diffed against them:
**identical**. So the `H` marks, the arities and the receiver bindings in
`help_lines/nodes.tsv` are what the dispatch table actually holds.

***The second half's seven rows were NOT re-measured, and that is a gap rather
than a decision.*** The enumerator the README describes is a throwaway that the
tree does not ship, so rebuilding it was not attempted on 2026-09-09. What the
rows claim is nonetheless checked from three directions: `install_hex_methods`
and `install_bits_methods` install every one of them as `{fn, true, 1}`, which
is the arity and the receiver binding the rows carry; `satl --words` prints all
fifteen paths and the `hexadecimal` alias from the built binary; and
`help_lines/gen.py` refuses to write `help.def` unless there is exactly one
entry per node of `words.def`, in `words.def` order — which is what caught the
renumber when hex's `as_number` came out. **A measurement would still
be better than three agreements**, and re-running it is owed the next time
anything touches this table.

**`make startup`: nothing regressed, measured after each half.** Every row
within noise of the baseline, both sides `STATIC=full`; after the second half
the largest movements were *downward* (`--limits`, −0.206 ms) and the largest
rise was +0.023 ms on bare `satl`. The measurement is PLAN §9's ask;
`make_support/startup.rows` is unchanged because there is nothing to record.

**Which is the expected answer and worth one line on why.** Both halves add
variant arms, `words.def` rows and handler installs — all of them work done once
at startup over a table that was already being built — so a cost that showed up
here would have meant something was being done per-value instead.

## 5. The second half — `satellite.variable.hex`, 2026-09-09

**It inherited everything §2 settled and re-argued none of it**: the width rule,
the equality rule, the four verbs, the byte-alignment refusal. That is the
split's return, and it is the reason this section is short.

**Stored as bits, per the author, and the arithmetic is why it costs nothing.**
One hex digit is exactly four bits, so the round trip is exact in both
directions and neither spelling is the "real" one — `x00FF` and
`b0000000011111111` are one run wearing two types. `HexRun` **holds** a `BitRun`
and does not derive from one, which `bits.hpp` had called before either
existed: under inheritance a `shared_ptr<const HexRun>` converts implicitly to
`shared_ptr<const BitRun>`, so a hex value could have been assigned into the
binary arm and silently become one.

**`Hex` is the EIGHTH append to `Value` and the 40-byte assert did not move**,
sixteen bytes against the 32-byte widest arm for the sixth time. It is a
separate arm rather than a radix flag on `Bin`, and that is what makes
`b1111 == xF` false without a line of code asking: different arms never compare
equal, which §8's model had already promised.

### 5.1 Three questions binary never had to answer

**`width()` counts BITS on both, and `digits()` is why nothing is lost.** The
author asked for both rows on 2026-09-09. `x00FF.width()` is 16 and
`x00FF.digits()` is 4. What decided it was a rule one module away rather than
taste: `write(x)`'s *"the width must be a multiple of eight"* then reads the
same sentence on both types. Had `width()` answered 4, that rule would silently
have become "a multiple of two" for hex while meaning one thing — and `digits()`
went onto **binary** too, where it always equals `width()`, so that the question
is askable of a value whose radix the program does not know.

**`as_number()` was built, shipped, and removed the same day** — §5.2 below is
the whole of it, and it is the one thing in this milestone that did not survive
its own commit.

**Case is not part of the value, and it is the one thing the two radices do not
share.** The lexer took `x00ff` and `x00FF` alike from M3, *"because the width is
what carries meaning and case does not"* — so storing the bits means the case is
not stored at all. `x00ff == x00FF` is **true**, and `display` therefore cannot
print back what was written the way it can for a bit run, whose digits *are* its
value. It prints upper, which is how DESIGN §8.5 spells every example it has.

### 5.2 `as_number()` on hex: built, then dropped hours later

**It answered 11111111 and the author read that answer.** `x00FF.as_number()`
→ 11111111 was in commit `cf04994`; the author's reply on seeing it was
*"x00FF.as_number() = 11111111 looks wrong to me"*, and the row came out.

**The row means "read these characters as an ordinary decimal", and hex has no
such reading.** Binary can answer only by an accident of alphabet: `0` and `1`
are also decimal digits, so `b1010`'s characters are the legal decimal 1010.
There is no decimal number spelled `00FF`. The version that shipped read the
BIT EXPANSION's characters instead — which made it total, and which is exactly
what is wrong with it: **11111111 is the decimal reading of characters no
program ever wrote.** One name over two different questions is worse than one
question with no name.

**The objection came in as being about leading zeros, and establishing that it
was not took most of a sitting.** It is worth writing down because the same
question will be asked again:

- **No conversion to a number can keep a leading zero, on either radix, ever.**
  §8.1's number has no width. `007 == 7` is **true** in satellite and was
  before either radix existed, and `b0011.as_number()` and `b11.as_number()`
  are both 11 — binary loses them exactly as hex did. Hand-writing a case per
  operation cannot help, because the destination has no slot to put a zero in.
- **The zeros are kept, in three places**: the value itself (`x00FF` displays
  `x00FF`), the width stored beside the bits (`width()` → 16), and the
  characters (`to_string()` → `"x00FF"`, `to_binary().to_string()` →
  `"b0000000011111111"`). The author's own instinct — *"store the zeroes
  somewhere ELSE... using width as a property I guess"* — describes what the
  type already does, and is their decision from the first half.
- **So the loss is the point of the type rather than a hole in it.** §8.5: *"the
  width is part of the value... That is the whole reason they are not number
  literals in another base."* If a number could hold `x0009`, hex would not
  need to exist.

**What it cost in numbering**: `digits` and `to_binary` moved to `1 6 11 4` and
`1 6 11 5`. Both had been minted hours earlier and seen by nothing outside this
tree, which is the only condition WORD_NUMBERS §1.2's *never renumber* permits
a shift under — **binary's `1 6 5 4` shipped the day before and is untouched.**

### 5.3 The two conversions are not symmetric, and that is the invariant

`hex.to_binary()` `1 6 11 6` can never fail. `binary.to_hex()` `1 6 5 6` refuses
a width that is not a multiple of four. **Both are the same fact seen from
opposite sides** — a hex value is built from digits and each is four bits, so the
width is always a multiple of four going out and needs checking coming in.
Padding three bits to four invents a bit the program never wrote, which is
`write(x)`'s multiple-of-eight refusal one step in and settled by the same
argument.

### 5.4 The two tables line up for three rows, then part

`1 6 5 n` and `1 6 11 n` ask the same question for n = 1, 2 and 3 — the value,
the width, the characters. **They part at 4**, because binary has `as_number`
and hex does not (§5.2), so hex's `digits` and `to_binary` sit at 4 and 5 where
binary's sit at 5 and 6.

**The alignment was arranged deliberately and then given up deliberately**, and
that is the better outcome: a shared number is worth having only when the two
rows ask the same question, and forcing hex to keep a row it could not answer
in order to preserve a tidy table would have been the numbering wagging the
language.

## 6. The operators: all decided, none built

**The author's call, 2026-09-09**: *"we are doing just too much at one time, and
we need to just pick a few things to do at a time"*. What made the size visible
was the author's own observation, arriving mid-specification — *"we forgot about
order of operations like completely"* — which is exactly right: a new operator
needs a precedence level, and `+` on two width-carrying types across four radix
combinations needs a carry rule, an answer-width and a story about mixed types.

**A method needs a `words.def` row and an operator does not**, which is the line
that made the delay free. Not one path number is spent by it. The answers are in
DESIGN §8.5 and §6.6 so the milestone that builds them starts from them:

| | decided |
|---|---|
| `+` | **adds**; `!!` **joins** |
| answer width | **grows to fit** — `xFF + x01` is `x100`; never wraps, never refuses |
| mixed radices | **allowed, left operand's type wins** — the language's first cross-type operation |
| `!!` precedence | **looser than `+`**, a new level 3; both fold left to right |
| `[i]` | **counts from the right** — `b0001[0]` is `b1` |
| the shifts | still **open**, the one question nobody has answered |

**Two of those reversed a written position and the reversals are the record.**
DESIGN §8.5 said index 0 was the *leftmost* bit until 2026-09-09, on the ground
that a string counts from the left; the author took the hardware convention
instead, and the cost — a subscript counting one way on a string and the other
on a bit run — is written into §8.5 rather than argued away. And the author's
first instruction on `!!` was *"right to left every time"*, which was scoped to
`!!` alone once the general form was measured: it would have made `10 - 3 - 2`
answer 9.

**`+` adding rather than joining was the author's, against §6.6's own
argument**, and the author named the cost while choosing it: *"I know people
will try to + for concatenate though"*. No compile-time signal can catch it —
both readings are legal programs — so what exists is the help text and one
accidental tell, that joining two 2-digit hexes is always 4 digits while adding
them is 2 or 3.
